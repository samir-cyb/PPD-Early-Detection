\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature-review}

This chapter reviews the existing research on postpartum depression (PPD) in Bangladesh and around the world. It looks at what has already been done, what is still missing, and why this thesis is needed. The chapter is organized into five parts: the clinical background of PPD in Bangladesh; a review of machine learning studies on PPD prediction; the research gaps this thesis addresses; a comparative summary of prior studies; the clinical grounding for the feature categories; and the justification for this thesis.

\section{Postpartum Depression in Bangladesh: Background and Burden}
\label{ch2-sec-background}

Postpartum depression is a well-known mental health condition that affects many mothers after childbirth. Globally, it affects between 10\% and 15\% of new mothers \cite{yazdkhasti2026systematic}. In Bangladesh, however, the numbers are much higher. A recent hospital-based study found that 47.78\% of new mothers showed symptoms of PPD, and about 29\% of them had major depression \cite{shirin2024burden}. Another study in urban slums of Dhaka found a prevalence of 39.4\% \cite{azad2019economic}. These numbers show that PPD is one of the most common health problems facing new mothers in Bangladesh.

The consequences of untreated PPD are serious. When a mother has PPD and does not receive help, she may struggle to bond with her baby, have difficulty taking care of herself and her child, and experience long-term mental health problems. In the most severe cases, PPD can lead to suicidal thoughts or actions. Despite this, most cases of PPD are never detected or treated.

There are several reasons why PPD is so often missed in Bangladesh:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lack of awareness.} Most Bangladeshi families do not know what PPD is. They may think that feeling sad after giving birth is normal, or they may blame the mother for being emotionally weak. Because of this, mothers do not seek help and families do not encourage them to \cite{shirin2024burden}.
    \item \textbf{Social stigma.} Mental health carries a strong stigma in Bangladesh. Women are often afraid to talk about their feelings because they worry about being judged by their family or community.
    \item \textbf{Shortage of mental health professionals.} Bangladesh has very few trained psychiatrists and counselors, especially outside of major cities.
    \item \textbf{Late and infrequent screening.} Even in hospitals, PPD screening is not done as a routine part of postnatal care. When screening does happen, it is usually at the six-to-eight-week follow-up visit, which is too late to catch the condition early.
\end{itemize}

The specific risk factors for PPD in Bangladesh are also different from those in high-income countries. Studies have found that the strongest predictors include domestic violence or abuse from the husband or in-laws \cite{islam2017ipv}; feeling angry or unhappy after childbirth; poor relationship with the husband; lack of family support; history of depression or anxiety before or during pregnancy; economic hardship; and health problems in the newborn \cite{yazdkhasti2026systematic, shirin2024burden}. These are not factors that a general global screening tool would automatically pick up, which is why a Bangladesh-specific model is needed.

\section{Machine Learning for PPD Prediction: Existing Work}
\label{ch2-sec-existing-work}

In the last several years, researchers have started using machine learning (ML) to predict PPD risk from electronic health records, government surveys, and questionnaire data. This section reviews the most relevant studies.

\subsection{Electronic Health Record Studies}

Some studies used data from hospitals and clinics to build their models. Zhang et al.\ trained a model on more than 30,000 patient records in the United States and reported an AUROC of 0.92 \cite{zhang2021development}. The same team later deployed their model in a real hospital system \cite{zhang2024implementation}. Amit et al.\ used UK primary care records and reported an AUC of 0.744 when tested on a later time period \cite{amit2021estimation}. Jeevitha and Priya also built an EHR-based model that used SHAP to explain its predictions \cite{jeevitha2025early}. These EHR-based models are useful but they require access to large, well-organized health record systems, which do not exist in most parts of Bangladesh.

\subsection{Large Survey Studies in Western Countries}

Shin et al.\ used the PRAMS survey from the United States, which has more than 35,000 responses, and found that Random Forest performed best with an AUC of 0.884 \cite{shin2020machine}. Andersson et al.\ used a cohort study from Sweden and reached an AUC of 0.81 \cite{andersson2021predicting}. Sibbald et al.\ used a Dutch cohort and reported a similar AUC \cite{sibbald2025identifying}. These studies show that ML can work well with survey data, but they were all done in countries with very different social and healthcare contexts from Bangladesh.

\subsection{Asian Studies}

Several studies from China used questionnaire data from hospitals and reported good model performance. Zhang et al.\ compared feature selection methods on 508 mothers and reached an AUC of 0.81 \cite{zhang2020machine}. More recent Chinese studies by Qi et al.\ \cite{qi2025prediction}, Huang et al.\ \cite{huang2025postpartum}, and Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2025interpretable} combined psychosocial and clinical features and reached AUCs above 0.80, with SHAP used to make the models interpretable. These studies are helpful reference points because they deal with survey-based data in an Asian healthcare context, similar to Bangladesh.

\subsection{Bangladeshi and South Asian Studies}

The most directly relevant pilot work is Raisa et al.\ \cite{raisa2022machine}, who surveyed 150 mothers in Bangladesh and reached 89\% accuracy using a Random Forest model. That study proved that a questionnaire-based ML approach could work in a Bangladeshi setting, and it directly motivated the collection of the larger 800-mother dataset used in this thesis.

A number of other studies from Bangladesh and similar South Asian populations report much higher accuracy, often above 97\% or even 99\%. Nasim et al.\ reported 99\% accuracy on a Bangladeshi dataset \cite{nasim2024novel}, Zohora reported 99.7\% accuracy \cite{zohora2024machine}, and Sreeji and Shirley reported close to 99\% accuracy \cite{sreeji2026predicting}. However, these studies do not apply systematic feature selection, use all questionnaire items indiscriminately, and several predict labels directly derived from those same items, which inflates the results.

The most directly comparable study to this thesis is by Chowdhury and Sultana \cite{chowdhury2026assessing}, who looked at the relationship between maternal age and PPD using explainable AI on a Bangladeshi dataset. This 2026 study demonstrated that ML with questionnaire data works in Bangladesh, but it did not apply feature selection, did not compare ML and deep learning models side by side, and did not build a deployable tool. This thesis directly extends and improves upon that work.

\textbf{Gap 1: Lack of systematic feature selection.}
No Bangladeshi PPD study has applied Chi-square feature ranking combined with backward elimination to identify which specific EPDS and PHQ-9 questionnaire items are most predictive for PPD risk classification. Most studies feed all questionnaire items into the model without selecting the optimal subset. This thesis fills the gap by applying principled feature selection and showing which 17 items are most important out of the original 65 features.

\textbf{Gap 2: No comprehensive ML vs.\ deep learning comparison.}
Most Bangladeshi PPD studies use only one or two models. None of them compare a wide range of both traditional ML models and deep learning models side by side on the same dataset. Without such a comparison, it is impossible to know whether deep learning actually helps or whether a simpler model like Gradient Boosting performs just as well. This thesis compares 15 models and provides a clear answer.

\textbf{Gap 3: Lack of SHAP explainability and a deployable tool.}
Most existing studies report only overall accuracy without explaining which questionnaire items drive predictions for each risk class. The few studies that use SHAP (such as Qi et al.\ \cite{qi2025prediction}) do not build a deployable web interface. This thesis adds both: a SHAP analysis using Permutation Explainer that identifies item-level drivers for each EPDS risk class, and a web interface for practical deployment.

\section{Clinical Grounding for Key Risk Factors}
\label{ch2-sec-clinical-grounding}

The four background feature categories used in this thesis are not chosen randomly. Each one is supported by clinical research that shows these categories are genuinely linked to PPD in Bangladesh and similar South Asian contexts.

\textbf{Demographic factors} such as age, education, income, and occupation are linked to PPD in Bangladesh because economic hardship and lower education are associated with reduced access to information, weaker support networks, and greater vulnerability to mental health problems. Azad et al.\ found a PPD prevalence of 39.4\% among the most economically deprived mothers in the urban slums of Dhaka \cite{azad2019economic}. These demographic features capture the socioeconomic context of each mother.

\textbf{Familial factors} including domestic violence, family type, marital relationship, and feelings about motherhood are among the strongest predictors of PPD in Bangladesh. Islam et al.\ found that lifetime intimate partner violence had an odds ratio of 3.48 for PPD in Bangladeshi urban slum mothers \cite{islam2017ipv}. Yazdkhasti et al.'s meta-analysis of more than 16,000 women found that poor husband relationships and lack of family support had an odds ratio of 4.41 for PPD \cite{yazdkhasti2026systematic}. The thesis confirms that anger after childbirth and feelings about motherhood are the strongest individual risk factors in this dataset.

\textbf{Personal Health factors} such as history of depression, anxiety, fear of pregnancy, and depression during pregnancy (PHQ-2) are well-established predictors of PPD across all populations. A mother who has experienced depression before or during her pregnancy is significantly more likely to develop PPD after childbirth \cite{yazdkhasti2026systematic}. These features capture a mother's mental health history and current psychological state.

\textbf{Neonatal Health factors} including delivery method, pregnancy complications, infant health, and postpartum support all contribute to PPD risk. Factors such as caesarean section, infant health problems, and insufficient postpartum support increase the risk of PPD \cite{moameri2019cesarean, shirin2024burden}. The neonatal category captures the birth experience and immediate postpartum context.

These clinical findings directly justify using the four background feature categories as comparison inputs alongside the selected EPDS and PHQ-9 items, and they provide an external check for the Kendall's tau rankings reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Why This Thesis is Needed}
\label{ch2-sec-justification}

Putting all of the above together, the justification for this thesis is straightforward.

Bangladesh has one of the highest rates of PPD in the world, yet no study on the 800-mother Bangladeshi dataset has applied systematic feature selection, a comprehensive model comparison, SHAP explainability, and a deployable web interface together. The existing high-accuracy studies from Bangladesh either use all questionnaire items without selection, or predict labels derived from those same items, and none of them explain which specific items are driving the predictions for each risk class.

The reference paper (Chowdhury and Sultana, 2026) demonstrated that ML works in Bangladesh, but it did not tell us which EPDS and PHQ-9 items are most informative, how ML compares to deep learning models, or how to deploy the result as a practical tool.

The feature selection finding in this thesis (17 optimal EPDS and PHQ-9 items identified from 65 features by Chi-square and backward elimination) is important because it reduces the questionnaire burden and identifies the specific items that matter most. The SHAP analysis adds a further layer: it shows which items drive predictions for Low, Medium, and High risk classes separately. The web interface makes the whole system usable in real clinical settings.

And finally, knowing \emph{that} a model can predict PPD is not enough. Healthcare workers also need to know \emph{which specific factors} put a mother at risk. The Kendall's tau analysis and within-category importance scores in this thesis provide exactly that: a clear, statistically grounded list of the most important background risk factors for PPD in Bangladeshi mothers, based on 800 real cases.

\section{Summary}

This chapter reviewed the clinical background of PPD in Bangladesh, summarised the existing machine learning literature on PPD prediction, identified three key gaps (lack of feature selection, no ML vs.\ DL comparison, no SHAP explainability or deployable tool), and grounded the four background feature categories in published clinical evidence. The next chapter describes the dataset and the full methodology in detail.
